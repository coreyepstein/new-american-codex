# Full Season Garden: Seed to Harvest

## Overview

You are going to grow food. Not a science-fair bean sprout in a cup — real food, in real dirt, across a real season, that ends up on your family's table. This is a field plan that runs from the first warm week of spring until the first frost of fall. You will plan it, plant it, defend it from weeds and bugs, and harvest it. Some of what you plant will thrive. Some of it will die, and you will figure out why. By the end, you will understand something most adults have forgotten: that food does not come from a store. It comes from soil, sun, water, and somebody who showed up every day.

This is not a project you finish in an afternoon. It is a commitment. The plants do not care whether you feel like watering. That is exactly the point.

## Location Requirements

Your garden is the field, and unlike a forest or a creek, you build it where you are. The single most important thing a garden needs is sun.

- **Type:** A spot that gets at least 6 hours of direct sunlight a day. Most vegetables — tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash — need full sun. Leafy greens like lettuce and spinach can handle a little less. Watch a potential spot for a full day and note when the sun hits it. Morning shade is fine; afternoon shade hurts.
- **Access:** Your own yard, a community garden plot, a balcony or patio with containers, or a school garden. No yard? Five-gallon buckets with drainage holes on a sunny balcony will grow tomatoes, peppers, beans, and herbs. The garden does not need to be in the ground.
- **Distance:** It must be close enough that you will actually visit it every single day. A garden across town that you see once a week will die. The best garden is the one you walk past on your way out the door.

A note on water: your garden must be within reach of a hose or close enough to carry water to. Hauling water 200 feet twice a day in July is how good gardeners quit.

## Pre-Trip Preparation

A garden lives or dies in the planning. Before you put a single seed in the ground, you make a map.

### Gear Checklist

- [ ] Garden bed or containers built and placed in a full-sun spot
- [ ] Soil and compost mixed and bed filled (soil should be loose, dark, and crumbly — not packed clay or pure sand)
- [ ] Seeds and seedlings chosen and bought (read the back of every seed packet)
- [ ] Trowel, cultivator, pruners, gloves, watering can
- [ ] Plant labels made out for every crop
- [ ] Garden journal set up with a planting map drawn in it
- [ ] Last-frost date for your region looked up (search "last frost date" plus your zip code)

### Knowledge Prep

Before you plant, you need to know three things, and the seed packet tells you most of them.

**Your frost dates.** Tender plants — tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash, cucumbers — die in frost. You cannot plant them outside until after your region's last spring frost. Cold-hardy plants — radishes, lettuce, peas, spinach — actually prefer cool weather and can go in weeks earlier. Look up your last-frost date and write it in your journal. That date is the starting gun for half your crops.

**Spacing.** Every seed packet lists how far apart to plant. This matters enormously. A tomato plant needs two feet of space. If you plant six tomatoes in a four-foot bed, they will choke each other, get no air, and rot. Beginners always plant too close. Trust the packet and resist the urge to cram.

**Days to maturity.** The packet tells you how many days from planting to harvest. Radishes: 25 days. Lettuce: 45 days. Tomatoes: 70-80 days from transplant. This is how you plan your season — and how you understand why you start tomatoes early and can sneak radishes in late.

**Make your map.** Draw your bed to scale in your journal. Put tall crops (tomatoes, pole beans) on the north side so they do not shade the short ones. Place the fast crops where you can easily replant after you harvest them. This map is your plan. You will mark it up all season.

## Field Schedule

A garden does not run on the clock of a single day — it runs on the clock of a season. Here is the schedule across the months. Adjust the timing to your region's frost dates.

| Time | Activity | Notes |
|------|----------|-------|
| Early spring (4-6 weeks before last frost) | Build and fill the bed; plant cold-hardy seeds (radish, lettuce, peas, spinach) directly | These tolerate cold soil and light frost |
| Last-frost week | Transplant tomatoes and peppers; direct-sow beans, squash, cucumbers | Wait for warm soil — cold dirt stalls warm crops |
| Late spring through summer | Daily watering, weekly weeding, scouting, thinning, staking | The long maintenance grind — this is the real work |
| Mid-summer | First harvests of fast crops; replant the empty space | Succession planting keeps the bed producing |
| Late summer | Peak harvest of tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash | Pick often — picking makes plants produce more |
| First fall frost | Final harvest; pull spent plants; record the season | Green tomatoes can ripen indoors on a windowsill |

## Observation Guide

A gardener is mostly an observer. The plants are constantly telling you what they need. Your job is to read them.

### Look For:

- **Wilting in the morning** means the plant needs water now. (Wilting in the heat of mid-afternoon is often normal — the plant rebounds in the evening. Check again at dusk before you panic.)
- **Yellowing lower leaves** can mean overwatering or a nutrient problem. Yellowing all over usually means too much water.
- **Holes in leaves** mean something is eating your plants — look on the undersides for caterpillars, beetles, or slug trails (shiny slime).
- **Crowding** — if seedlings come up in a thick clump, they must be thinned (more on that below).
- **Ripeness signs** — a tomato that gives slightly to a gentle squeeze and is fully colored; a bean pod that is firm and snaps; lettuce leaves big enough to eat before the plant "bolts" (sends up a tall flower stalk and turns bitter).

### Record:

Every visit, jot a quick line in your garden journal: date, what you did (watered, weeded, harvested), and anything you noticed. Once a week, write more: which plants are thriving, which are struggling, what the weather has been. Photos are excellent data — take one of the whole bed every week from the same spot and you will be amazed at the time-lapse.

### Questions to Investigate:

- Why did the seeds you direct-sowed do better or worse than the transplants?
- Which crop gave the most food for the space it took? (This is the start of thinking like a farmer.)
- What killed the plants that died — too little water, too much, bugs, disease, or crowding?
- If you planted the same crop again, what one thing would you change?

## Post-Trip Processing

At the end of the season, you do not just walk away. You harvest the knowledge along with the last of the vegetables.

Pull out your journal and write a one-page season report. What did you plant? What did you actually harvest, and roughly how much? Weigh it if you can — a kitchen scale and a running tally turns "we got some beans" into "we grew 4.2 pounds of green beans from a one-dollar seed packet." That number is the economics of growing your own food, and it is worth knowing.

Then do the most valuable thing a gardener does: plan next year. Based on what worked, what would you grow more of? What would you cut? Where would you move the tomatoes? This is how a one-season garden becomes a lifelong skill — each year informed by the last.

Compost or pull the spent plants, and if your soil allowed it, you have just completed the oldest human technology there is: turning seed into food.

## Weather & Season Notes

- **Frost is the master switch.** Cold-hardy crops go in early and survive light frost. Warm crops wait until all danger of frost has passed, period. A late frost will kill tomatoes you put out too soon.
- **Heat waves** mean water in the early morning, not midday (midday water evaporates and can scorch leaves). A deep soak every other day beats a light sprinkle every day — deep watering trains roots to grow down.
- **Drought** is when daily attention pays off. Mulch (straw, shredded leaves, grass clippings) around plants holds moisture and smothers weeds.
- **The first fall frost ends the warm season.** Watch the forecast in early autumn. The night before a hard frost, pick everything that is close to ripe.
- **Rain is free water** — skip your watering on rainy days, and feel a small thrill that the sky did your chore.

## Safety Notes

This field plan is rated **yellow** — an adult must facilitate setup, tool use, and any product handling, and supervise the first sessions until you have demonstrated safe habits.

### Hazards

- **Hand tools.** Trowels and cultivators have points; pruners are sharp. Carry them pointing down, never run with them, and put them back in one designated spot so nobody kneels on a hidden trowel. An adult must demonstrate pruner use before you cut anything.
- **Sun and heat.** Garden work in summer means sun exposure and dehydration. Wear a hat, use sunscreen, keep a water bottle at the bed, and work in the cooler morning or evening hours. Stop and go inside if you feel dizzy or overheated.
- **Soil amendments and bait.** If you use any fertilizer, slug bait, or compost amendment, an adult handles and stores it. Read the label. Many slug baits are poisonous to pets and must be used only as directed. Prefer organic, child-safe options.
- **Always wash your hands and wash your harvest.** Soil carries bacteria. Gloves on while working; hands washed after; every vegetable rinsed before eating. Never eat anything straight off the plant without washing — and never eat a plant or berry you cannot positively identify.
- **Bees and stinging insects** love a flowering garden, which is a good thing — they pollinate your crops. Move calmly around them and they will ignore you. If you are allergic to stings, tell the adult and keep your kit nearby.

### Emergency Plan

- **Nearest help:** An adult should be within earshot during work sessions. Know where the first aid kit is.
- **Communication:** Keep a phone accessible if working in a community plot away from home.
- **Bail-out plan:** If a tool causes a cut, stop, apply pressure, and get an adult. If you start feeling overheated, sick, or dizzy, stop immediately, get into shade, and drink water. The garden will still be there tomorrow — your health comes first, always.

### Rules

- Tools go back to their spot every single time, point down.
- Gloves on for soil work; hands washed after every session.
- An adult is present and aware whenever you are using pruners or handling any garden product.
- Wash everything before it goes in your mouth. No exceptions.
