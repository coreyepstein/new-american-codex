# Design and Prototype: From Sketch to CAD to a Working Object

## Overview

Anyone can have an idea. The skill that separates a daydreamer from a maker is the ability to drive an idea all the way from a vague "wouldn't it be cool if" to a physical object you can hold, test, and fix. That path — define the need, sketch it, model it precisely in CAD, fabricate it, test it against reality, and revise — is the core loop of engineering and product design. In this project you will run that loop, end to end, on a real problem worth solving.

The object you make matters less than the discipline you build. Your first prototype will be wrong in ways you did not anticipate, because the real world is the only honest critic of a design. A part that looked perfect on screen will be too loose, too weak, or impossible to assemble. That is not a setback. That is the entire reason prototypes exist — to find the flaws cheaply, before they become expensive. By the end you will have a working object and, more valuable, the proof that you can take any idea through the whole gauntlet from sketch to thing.

## The Deliverable

A working physical prototype that solves a real, specific problem, plus a design journal documenting the full process. "Done" looks like this: you can hold the object, it does the job you designed it to do, and your journal shows the sketches, the CAD model, the test results, and at least two iterations where you found a flaw and fixed it. A first version that works on the first try is not the goal — and is usually a sign you picked too easy a problem. The goal is a final version that works *because* you fixed what the earlier versions got wrong.

Good problems to design for:
- A custom bracket or mount for a specific object (a phone stand for your desk, a holder for a tool that has no home)
- An organizer for a real, measured space (a drawer insert, a cable manager, a part tray)
- A replacement part for something broken where the original is unavailable
- A jig or fixture that makes one of your own building tasks repeatable
- An adapter that connects two things that were never meant to connect

The best problem is one *you* actually have, because you will know immediately whether the prototype solves it.

And a hand-built prototype in cardboard, foam, or wood teaches the same design loop — professional designers build cardboard mockups constantly before committing to expensive fabrication.

## Project Phases

### Phase 1: Define and Sketch (Sessions 1-2)

#### Write the requirements first

Before you sketch anything, write down what the object must do, in specific and testable terms. This is the step beginners skip and engineers never do. Vague requirements produce vague objects. Instead of "a good phone stand," write:

- Holds a phone of these exact dimensions (measure yours with calipers)
- Holds it at roughly a 60-degree angle for video calls
- Does not tip when the screen is tapped
- Fits in the 90mm of depth available on my desk shelf

Each of those is something you can later test the prototype against. Requirements are the ruler you will measure success with, so write them before you fall in love with any particular design.

#### Sketch on paper, fast and many

Now sketch — by hand, in pencil, quickly, and several different concepts. Do not polish. The point of sketching is to think with your hand and generate options, not to make art. Draw five different ways to solve the problem. Most will be bad. One or two will have a good idea in them. Sketching is cheap; CAD is slower and a 3D print is slower still, so do your worst thinking on paper where mistakes cost seconds.

Pick the strongest concept, then sketch it again with real dimensions, measured from the actual objects it must fit. This dimensioned sketch is your bridge to CAD.

### Phase 2: Model in CAD (Sessions 3-5)

CAD — computer-aided design — lets you build a precise 3D model of your part that the fabrication machine can turn into a real object. Start in a program matched to your level. Tinkercad assembles shapes like digital blocks and is the fastest way to make something printable. Onshape and Fusion are parametric — you draw exact sketches with real dimensions and constraints, then extrude and modify them — and they are what real engineers use, with a steeper but worthwhile learning curve.

**Milestone 1: Learn the program on a throwaway.** Before modeling your real part, follow one beginner tutorial for your chosen software and model something simple — a box with a hole, a bracket. Spend a whole session just learning to navigate, sketch, extrude, and measure. Fighting the software and designing your part at the same time is twice as hard as doing them in sequence.

**Milestone 2: Model your part to real dimensions.** Now build your actual design, using the measurements from your dimensioned sketch. Model it accurately — if the phone is 71.5mm wide, the slot is 71.5mm plus a deliberate clearance, not "about 72." Precision in CAD is free; learn to use it. Pay attention to *tolerances and fits*: two parts that must slide together cannot be modeled at exactly the same size, or they will jam. Real parts need a gap — often a few tenths of a millimeter — and learning how much is a skill you build by testing.

**Milestone 3: Check it before you fabricate.** Rotate the model, view it from every angle, and mentally walk through assembling and using it. Is anything impossibly thin and likely to snap? Does a 3D printer have to print a flat shelf hanging in mid-air with nothing under it (an "overhang" that needs support or a redesign)? Can the part actually come off the printer bed or out of the laser cutter? Catching these on screen costs nothing; catching them after a four-hour print costs four hours.

### Phase 3: Fabricate and Test (Sessions 6-8)

**Fabricate the first prototype.** Export your model in the right format (STL for 3D printing, a vector file like DXF or SVG for laser cutting) and make the part. If 3D printing, learn what the slicer software does — it translates your model into the layer-by-layer instructions the printer follows, and its settings (layer height, infill, supports) affect strength and print time. If hand-building, transfer your CAD dimensions to your material and cut carefully.

**Test against your requirements — honestly.** Pull out the requirements list from Phase 1 and check each one. Does it hold the phone? At the right angle? Without tipping? Does it fit the shelf? Write down exactly what passes and what fails, and *why* it fails. "The slot is too tight and the phone won't seat" is a useful test result. "It's not great" is not. The first prototype almost always fails at least one requirement — that is expected and it is the point.

### Phase 4: Iterate (Sessions 8-10) and Present

**Iterate at least twice.** Take a specific failure, return to the CAD model, and change exactly what caused it. Slot too tight? Widen it by the measured amount. Tips over? Widen the base or move the weight. Then fabricate again and re-test. This is the heart of the whole project: a tight loop of model, make, test, diagnose, fix. Each iteration should fix a named, measured problem — not just "make it better." Two real iterations is the minimum; serious designs go through many more.

**Present the final object and the process.** Show your finished prototype to someone — a parent, a mentor, a maker group — but present the *process*, not just the object. Walk them through the requirements, show the original sketch, the CAD model, the failed first print sitting next to the working final one, and explain what each iteration fixed. The failed prototypes are not embarrassing; lined up next to the working one, they are the most impressive part, because they prove you can engineer, not just guess lucky.

## A Walkthrough: One Bracket, Three Versions

Here is what the design loop actually feels like, so the phases above are concrete rather than abstract.

You want a wall bracket to hold a heavy flashlight by your back door, because it keeps rolling off the shelf. You start with requirements, measured: the flashlight body is 38mm in diameter, it weighs about 400 grams, the bracket must screw into a single stud, and it must release the light with one hand in the dark. You sketch five concepts on paper in ten minutes — a closed ring, an open C-clip, a cradle, a magnetic mount, a two-finger clip. The magnetic mount is clever but you have no magnet on hand, so you pick the open C-clip: simple, one-handed, printable.

You model it in CAD. The C-clip's inner diameter is the part that matters, so you set it to 38mm — exactly the flashlight's diameter — and extrude it, add a back plate with a screw hole, and admire it. You print it. It comes out clean. You try to clip the flashlight in and it will not go: the opening is rigid and the clip, sized at exactly 38mm, has zero room to flex over the body. That is your first failure, and it is a specific, named one — *no clearance and no flex.* You write it down.

Version two: you open the model, widen the inner diameter to 40mm so it is not a press-fit, and thin the C-clip's walls slightly so the plastic can spring open as the light goes in. You print again. Now the flashlight clips in — but when you tap it, the whole bracket flexes and the light pops out. The walls are now *too* thin. Second failure, also named: *over-corrected the flex and lost retention.* This is the loop working exactly as it should — each version trades one problem for the next, and you are converging.

Version three: you keep the 40mm opening but add a small lip at the mouth of the C so the flashlight has to push past it to come out, and you thicken the back of the clip where it does not need to flex while keeping the mouth springy. Print, test against every requirement: clips in one-handed, holds when tapped, screws to a stud, releases with a deliberate pull in the dark. It passes. Three versions, two named failures, one working object — and the two failed prints sitting on your desk are the proof that you engineered it instead of guessing.

## Success Criteria

- [ ] The final prototype meets every requirement written in Phase 1, verified by actual testing
- [ ] There is a real CAD model, dimensioned accurately from measurements of the real-world objects involved
- [ ] At least two iterations occurred, each fixing a specific, named flaw found by testing
- [ ] The design journal documents requirements, sketches, the CAD model, test results, and the reasoning behind each change
- [ ] The student can explain a tolerance or fit decision — why a gap was needed and how big they made it
- [ ] The student can present the full process to someone else and justify the design choices

## Common Pitfalls

- **Skipping the requirements and sketches to jump straight into CAD.** This produces an object that looks finished but solves the wrong problem. Define and sketch first, every time. The five minutes of requirements saves hours of remodeling.
- **Modeling parts at exactly the same size and expecting them to fit.** Two surfaces modeled identically will jam in the real world. Build in clearance, then test and adjust it. This is the number-one reason a beginner's first assembly fails.
- **Treating the first prototype as the final answer.** If your first print works perfectly, you probably picked too simple a problem or got lucky. Real design is iteration. Plan from the start to make it twice.
- **Letting the software become the project.** It is easy to spend ten hours fighting CAD and forget you are solving a real problem. If the tool is blocking you, simplify the design or switch to an easier program. The goal is a working object, not mastery of a particular menu.
- **Designing something the machine cannot make.** A model with razor-thin walls, huge unsupported overhangs, or impossible geometry looks fine on screen and fails on the printer. Learn your fabrication method's limits and design within them.

## Extensions

- **Make it assemblable.** Design a two-or-more-part object where the pieces fit together — a box with a sliding lid, a part that snaps in. Multi-part assemblies force you to master tolerances, which is where real mechanical design lives.
- **Learn parametric design properly.** If you started in Tinkercad, rebuild your part in Onshape or Fusion using true parametric sketches, where changing one dimension updates the whole model. This is how professional CAD works and it makes iteration dramatically faster.
- **Design for someone else.** Find a real person with a real "I wish I had a thing that..." problem, interview them about their requirements, and design to *their* spec. Designing to your own needs is good practice; designing to someone else's is the actual job.
- **Reverse-engineer a part.** Take a broken plastic part from a household object, measure it with calipers, model it in CAD, and print a replacement that actually works. This is one of the most satisfying and genuinely useful things a maker can do.

## Safety Notes

This project is rated **yellow** — the design and CAD work is harmless, but fabrication involves machines and tools that require respect and adult awareness.

- **3D printers run hot.** The nozzle reaches well over 200°C (hot enough to cause a serious burn instantly) and the bed is often hot too. Never touch the nozzle or reach into a printing machine. Let parts and the bed cool before removing prints. Some filaments emit fumes — run the printer in a ventilated space.
- **Removing prints and cleaning up supports** often involves a sharp tool — a scraper or flush cutters. Cut and scrape *away* from your body and hands, and wear safety glasses, because snapped-off support material flies.
- **Laser cutters are red-level machines and require trained supervision.** A laser cutter can cause serious eye injury and start fires, and the cutting process produces fumes that must be vented. Only operate one under the direct supervision of a trained adult (typically a makerspace or shop staff member), follow that facility's rules exactly, and never leave a running laser unattended.
- **Hand fabrication uses cutting tools.** If you build your prototype by hand, the standard tool-safety rules apply: sharp blades, cuts away from the body, work secured, eye protection on.
- **Know your facility's rules.** Makerspaces and library fab labs have safety training and operating rules for a reason. Complete their training, ask before using an unfamiliar machine, and never run equipment you have not been checked out on. Part of being a real maker is respecting the shop you are a guest in.
