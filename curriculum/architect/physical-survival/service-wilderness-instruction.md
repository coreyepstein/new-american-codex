# Wilderness Instruction

## Overview

You are going to teach a group of younger students — Explorer or Builder stage — a real set of outdoor and survival skills, over a sequence of sessions you design and run yourself, culminating in an overnight where they put it all together. Not a one-off demo. A program: fire, shelter, knife and tool use, navigation, water treatment, and the judgment that ties them together, taught in a deliberate order, practiced under your eye, and tested in the field.

This is service because you are giving younger people something most of them will not otherwise get: a competent near-peer who can show them how to be safe and capable outdoors, and who remembers what it felt like to not know any of it yet. It is also one of the fastest ways there is to find out whether *you* actually know what you think you know. You can fool yourself about your own competence on a solo trip, where you only ever do the things you are already good at. You cannot fool eight twelve-year-olds who will ask you why, who will do exactly the wrong thing the instant your attention drifts, and who will replicate your bad habits with perfect fidelity. Teaching is the most honest mirror your skills will ever face.

The job here is genuinely two jobs running at once, and that is the hard part. You are an instructor — sequencing skills, demonstrating cleanly, giving feedback — and you are simultaneously the safety officer for a group of children in an environment with real hazards. Most first-time instructors can do either one alone and lose the other under load: they get absorbed in teaching a beautiful fire lay and stop counting heads, or they get so locked onto safety that the teaching collapses into "don't touch that." Holding both at once, calmly, is the actual skill this project builds.

## The Need

Most kids today reach adolescence having never been taught to be safe and self-reliant outdoors by anyone. The knowledge that used to pass down through scouting, through extended family, through a neighborhood full of older kids who knew things, has thinned out to almost nothing in a generation. The result is a population of young people who are either kept entirely indoors by adults who are themselves uncomfortable outside, or who go outside with no skills and no judgment and get hurt in entirely preventable ways — hypothermic in 50-degree rain because nobody taught them that cotton kills, lost a quarter mile from the trailhead because nobody taught them to keep track of where they are, burned because nobody ever showed them how fire actually behaves.

That is the gap you fill, and you fill it from a position an adult instructor cannot quite occupy. To a twelve-year-old, an adult survival instructor is a different species — competent in a way that feels unreachable, "of course *he* can build a fire, he's a grown-up." You are not a different species. You are close enough that your competence reads to them as *attainable*: if someone only a few years older can do this, so can I, eventually. That belief — *this is learnable, and people like me learn it* — is the most valuable thing you hand them, more valuable than any single skill, and you can hand it precisely because of how recently you learned it yourself.

## Civic Connection

A self-reliant population is a free one. People who can take care of themselves and each other in the physical world — who can stay warm, find their way, treat a wound, keep a head when something goes wrong — are harder to panic, harder to make helpless, and quicker to help a neighbor than to wait for someone official to arrive. Every kid you teach to be capable outdoors is one more person in your community who, in a bad moment, will be a source of calm rather than a source of need.

There is a longer chain at work too. The skills you are passing down were passed to you, and almost certainly not by someone who charged for it — by a parent, a scoutmaster, a mentor, an older kid who decided you were worth the time. Teaching a younger group is how you take your place in that chain instead of letting it end with you. The most durable form of Tikkun Olam in the physical-skills world is not the rescue you personally perform someday; it is the dozen capable people you created who go on to keep themselves and others safe for decades, and who teach the next dozen. You are not just running a program. You are seeding competence that propagates.

## Planning

### Who You're Serving

- **Organization/community:** A homeschool co-op, a younger group at a program you attend, a cluster of neighborhood families, a younger den or troop, or simply a handful of younger kids whose parents trust you. Four to eight students is the right size — enough for a real group dynamic, few enough that you can see every one of them at every moment.
- **Contact person:** The parents or guardians, collectively. Their written consent, their awareness of every session's plan and location, and the presence of at least one of them (or another responsible adult) at every session are all non-negotiable. You are leading minors in an environment with hazards; this is set up with full parental knowledge or it does not happen.
- **Coordination needed:** Agree on a recurring time and a fixed, vetted location. Collect a roster with each child's emergency contact, allergies, and medical conditions (asthma, diabetes, severe allergies — you must know before you light a fire or hand out a snack). Confirm who the on-site adult is for each session. Establish the weather-cancellation rule in advance so no parent is guessing.

### What You'll Do

- Design a sequenced curriculum that builds from the safest skills to the most consequential, never out of order
- Demonstrate each skill cleanly, then coach the students through doing it themselves
- Manage the group and the environment simultaneously — teaching while never losing track of where every child is and what every child is touching
- Assess mastery by watching the students actually perform the skill, not by asking if they understood
- Lead a culminating overnight where the group uses the skills together, under your supervision, for real

### What You'll Need

- A written lesson plan for each session, with the skill, the steps, the hazards, and the safety brief spelled out before you arrive
- Demonstration gear that *you* control and hand out deliberately — students do not have free access to knives, fire, or saws
- The first aid kit on your body at every session, and a water source or extinguisher at every fire
- A clear-eyed sense of your own limits: you teach only what you have genuinely mastered, and you say "I don't know, let's find out" rather than improvising on something consequential

### Schedule

| Date/Time | Activity | Location |
|-----------|----------|----------|
| Weeks 1-2 | Design the curriculum; vet the site; collect rosters and consent | Home / proposed site |
| Session 1 | Outdoor mindset, the seven priorities of survival, knot tying (no sharps, no fire) | Backyard / park |
| Session 2 | Knife and saw safety; carving simple tent stakes and feather sticks | Vetted woods / park |
| Session 3 | Shelter — tarp and natural-material construction; staying dry and warm | Vetted woods |
| Session 4 | Fire — fuel staging, fire lays, ignition with a ferro rod, and putting it dead out | Vetted site with water on hand |
| Session 5 | Navigation — map orientation, baseplate compass, keeping track of position | Park with varied terrain |
| Session 6 | Water — finding, filtering, and treating; the danger of untreated water | Near a water source |
| Culminating | Supervised overnight — the group runs the full skill set together | Established campground / vetted site |

## Before You Begin

The setup determines whether this works and whether it is safe, so do it deliberately and in full before the first session.

First, fix the sequence, because the order is not arbitrary — it is a safety decision. You teach skills in ascending order of consequence. Knots and mindset first: a student cannot get hurt tying a bowline, and the early sessions are where you learn each child's temperament, attention, and trustworthiness before you ever put a sharp or a flame in their hands. Then sharps, then shelter, then fire, then navigation and water. By the time you reach fire — the most consequential thing you will teach — you already know which kids follow instructions, which ones are impulsive, and where you need to stand. A first-time instructor's instinct is to lead with the exciting stuff (fire! knives!) because it holds attention. Resist it. The boring, safe early sessions are how you earn the right to teach the dangerous ones.

Second, vet the site yourself, in advance, on foot. Walk it before any child sees it. Find the hazards a twelve-year-old will find: the steep drop they will run toward, the water they will wade into, the dead branches overhead, the road at the edge, the poison ivy, the broken glass. Identify exactly where you will run the fire session and confirm there is water there. Know the nearest road, the nearest place an ambulance could reach, and how you would get an injured child out. You cannot supervise hazards you have not located.

Third, accept that you teach only what you own. This is the discipline that separates a real instructor from someone playing one. If your own fire-starting is inconsistent in damp conditions, do not teach damp-weather fire to children as if you have it mastered — teach what you actually know, cold and reliably, and be honest about the edges of your competence. Children copy what you do exactly, including your shortcuts and your bad habits. Whatever sloppiness you have tolerated in your own practice, they will inherit and amplify. The act of preparing to teach a skill is the moment to clean up your own technique to a standard you would be willing to see reflected back at you by eight beginners.

### Reading the Two Failure Modes

Everything that goes wrong in field instruction is a slide toward one of two poles, and the correction is different for each.

**Teaching and forgetting to watch.** You get absorbed — in a beautiful demonstration, in coaching one struggling kid, in your own enjoyment of the skill — and the group dissolves at the edges of your attention. A child wanders toward the water. Two kids start an unsupervised contest with their knives. The fire creeps into the leaf litter while you are explaining the next step. This is the dangerous failure, because the environment does not wait for you to notice. The signal that you are sliding into it: you cannot, at this exact second, name where every child is and what is in their hands. The correction is a discipline borrowed from professional outdoor educators — *count heads on a rhythm.* Every few minutes, regardless of what you are teaching, your eyes do a full sweep and you account for every student and every hazard. When you put a dangerous tool in play, you teach to the whole group's hands at once and you do not turn your back on a lit fire or an open blade. Safety is not a section of the lesson. It is the continuous background process that never pauses.

**Telling instead of teaching.** The opposite slide. You know the skill so well that you explain it — at length, accurately, uselessly. The students nod, understand nothing in their hands, and cannot do it when you stop talking. Physical skills do not transfer through words; they transfer through demonstration and corrected repetition. The signal you are sliding into this: you are talking more than they are doing, or you find yourself answering "do you understand?" with a chorus of yeses that mean nothing. The correction is the oldest teaching sequence there is — *I do, we do, you do.* You demonstrate the skill cleanly and silently first so they can see the whole shape of it. Then you do it again, narrating the steps. Then they do it, slowly, while you watch and correct one thing at a time. Then they do it until it is theirs. Assessment is never "did you get it?" — it is "show me," and you believe your eyes, not their answer.

The whole craft is holding both at once: a skill being genuinely transferred into young hands, while every one of those hands stays accounted for and safe.

## During Service

### Tasks

1. **Open every session with the safety brief, every time.** Before any teaching, state the hazards of today's skill, the rules, and the boundary of the area. Repetition is not condescension — it is how a safety culture forms. The day you skip it because "they know" is the day someone forgets.
2. **Demonstrate before you explain.** Show the skill clean and whole before you break it into words. Let them see what "good" looks like, then teach the steps that get there. Narration follows demonstration; it never replaces it.
3. **Coach one correction at a time.** When a student does it wrong, do not list five errors. Fix the one that matters most for safety or success, let them try again, then fix the next. A beginner can only absorb one correction at a time, and the most important one is almost always the one that keeps them from getting hurt.
4. **Keep the group accounted for continuously.** Run your head-count rhythm. Never turn your back on a fire or a blade in use. Position yourself where you can see the most kids and the worst hazard at the same time. This is the job under the job, and it never stops.
5. **Assess by watching them perform.** A student has "got" a skill when they can do it correctly without you talking. Track who has reached that bar on each skill, because the overnight depends on it — you are not taking anyone into the field on a skill they have only nodded along to.

### Learning Moments

- Notice the exact instant your attention narrows to one student and the group goes dark. The first time you "come up for air" mid-lesson and realize you have no idea where two kids are, study that feeling. That tunnel-vision is the instructor's most dangerous instinct, and learning to teach with a wide aperture — fully present with one student while still tracking all of them — is the central skill of group field leadership. It is trainable, and the only way to train it is to catch yourself losing it.
- Watch your own competence get exposed. The first time a sharp ten-year-old asks "but *why* does the fire need air from underneath?" and you discover you have been doing it right by habit without actually understanding it, do not paper over it. That gap — the thing you can do but cannot explain — is real, and the question just found it for you. Teaching converts unconscious competence into conscious competence, and conscious competence is the only kind you can rely on under pressure or pass on cleanly.
- Pay attention to the difference between a kid who is quiet because they understand and a kid who is quiet because they are lost and embarrassed. The lost ones go silent and disappear to the back; the loudest learner is rarely the one in trouble. Learning to find the silent struggler — and to make it safe for them to be a beginner in front of peers — is a teaching skill that will serve you in every domain, not just the woods.

## After Service

### Reflection

- What need did you address? Be precise: you gave a specific group of younger kids a specific set of real, safety-relevant skills and the belief that they can be capable outdoors. That is small in number and large in consequence — these are skills that can matter to them for life, and in a bad moment, save it.
- How did it feel to contribute? Notice the difference between the satisfaction of being looked up to as the competent one and the deeper satisfaction of watching a kid build their first solid fire entirely on their own. Only the second is the point. The first is a trap.
- What did you learn about this community/issue — and about yourself? Where did teaching expose a gap in your own competence? Where did you slide toward forgetting to watch, or toward telling instead of teaching? What did running a group teach you about your own outdoor judgment that solo trips never had?
- What would make this service more effective? What sequence would you change? Which skill did you under-teach? Where was the group nearly unsafe, and what system would prevent that next time?
- Would you do this again, and would you go bigger — a longer program, a harder skill set, a real expedition with this group as they grow?

### Follow-Through

- Tell each parent, specifically, what you saw their child do. "She built a fire from scratch in the rain on the overnight, completely on her own" is a gift to a parent and confirms that the trust they extended you was sound.
- Leave the group with the means to keep practicing — a simple skills checklist, a knot card, a recommended next step — so the competence does not evaporate the week after the program ends.
- Consider whether this becomes a recurring thing. Some of these kids will want to keep going, and a program that runs every season, deepening as the students grow, is a far larger contribution than a single six-week course. The chain continues if you let it.

## Impact Measurement

The honest measure of wilderness instruction is not how well *you* performed — it is what the students can now do without you. The first and clearest signal is on the overnight: can each child execute the core skills correctly with you standing back and saying nothing? A kid who builds a safe fire, treats their own water, and stays warm and dry through a cold night, entirely on their own, is the whole product of the work. Beyond the overnight, watch for the durable change — do they go outside *more* now, with more confidence and better judgment? Do they teach a sibling what you taught them? The deepest sign you taught well is the same paradox that defines all good teaching: they need you less. A group of younger people who are now genuinely safe and capable in the outdoors — and who carry the belief that they are the kind of people who handle the physical world competently — is a contribution that keeps compounding in them, and in everyone they go on to keep safe, long after they have forgotten exactly which session taught them what.

## Safety Notes

This is a yellow-level service program: it involves children, sharp tools, fire, and an outdoor environment with real hazards. The safety burden is on you, the instructor, and it is continuous.

**Non-negotiable requirements:**

- **An adult co-leader or parent is present at every single session.** You are a capable young person, not a licensed guardian. A responsible adult is on site at all times, knows the plan, and is the backstop if you are the one who gets hurt. There are no exceptions to this, including the overnight.
- **You carry the first aid kit on your body, every session.** Not in a car, not in a pack across the clearing — on you. It includes wound care, burn dressings, an irrigation source for eyes and wounds, gloves, and any rescue medication a participant requires (an inhaler, an epinephrine auto-injector). You know, from the roster, who needs what before you begin.
- **Fire sessions require water or an extinguisher within arm's reach and a fire that is put dead out** — drowned, stirred, and cold to the touch — before anyone leaves. You teach fire only where it is legal and safe, check local fire restrictions and burn bans first, and never light a fire in dry, windy conditions or in dry-grass fuels.
- **Sharps are controlled, not free.** You hand out knives and saws deliberately, teach the blood-circle rule (arm's length of clear space around anyone using a blade — no one inside it), insist on a stable carving position with the blade moving away from the body, and collect the tools when the skill segment ends. Beginners do not roam with open blades.
- **You teach only what you have genuinely mastered.** Do not improvise consequential skills in front of children. "I don't know — we'll learn it properly before next session" is a complete, honest, and safe answer.

**Environment:** Vet the site on foot before the children arrive. Brief the group on the area boundary at every session and enforce it. Watch for the predictable hazards — water, drops, dead overhead branches, traffic, poison ivy and oak, ticks, biting and stinging insects, and weather. Check the forecast and cancel for lightning, severe weather, or extreme heat or cold without hesitation; a canceled session is always cheaper than an injured child.

**Communication:** Carry a charged phone or a radio. Before each session, know the nearest road an ambulance can reach, the address or coordinates you would give a 911 dispatcher, and the route you would use to evacuate an injured child. Have every parent's contact information on the roster, in hand, at every session.

**Overnight-specific:** The culminating overnight raises the stakes — cold, dark, and fatigue compound every other hazard. Choose an established, accessible site for it. Keep the adult-to-child ratio appropriate. Have a warming plan and dry spare layers for anyone who gets wet or cold, and know the early signs of hypothermia (shivering, clumsiness, slurred or sluggish behavior) and that the fix is immediate — dry, warm, calories, shelter. The night a child gets quietly cold while everyone else is having fun is the night this matters most.
